# WIRELESS TECHNOLOGY — MODEL SOLUTIONS Department of Electronics and Computer Science | Modules 1–2 --- ## MODULE 1 — Introduction to Wireless Communication ### 2-Mark Questions Q1. Define wireless communication. What is the basic communication system model? - Wireless communication is the transfer of information between two or more points without a physical (wired) connection, using electromagnetic waves as the carrier. - Basic model: Information Source → Transmitter → Channel (with noise) → Receiver → Destination. - Example: A mobile phone call, where voice is converted to an electrical signal, modulated, transmitted through air, and demodulated at the receiver. Q2. What is the wireless spectrum? Why is spectrum regulation important? - The wireless spectrum is the range of electromagnetic frequencies (typically a few kHz to several GHz) used to transmit wireless signals. - It is a finite, shared natural resource — only a limited number of frequency bands are usable for communication. - Regulation (by bodies like ITU, TRAI/FCC) prevents interference between users/services and ensures fair, efficient allocation. - Example: Cellular operators are allotted specific bands (e.g., 900 MHz, 1800 MHz) to avoid overlap with TV or defense frequencies. Q3. Differentiate between PAN, LAN, MAN, and WAN. - PAN (Personal Area Network): very short range (\~10 m), e.g., Bluetooth, Zigbee. - LAN (Local Area Network): covers a building/campus (\~100 m–1 km), e.g., Wi-Fi. - MAN (Metropolitan Area Network): covers a city (\~few km–50 km), e.g., WiMAX. - WAN (Wide Area Network): covers countries/continents, e.g., cellular networks, satellite links. Q4. List any four challenges in wireless communication. - Limited and shared spectrum availability. - Multipath fading and signal attenuation over distance. - Interference from other wireless sources and noise. - Security vulnerabilities (eavesdropping, jamming) due to open-air transmission. Q5. Trace the evolution of wireless systems from 1G to current generations. - 1G (1980s): Analog voice-only communication (AMPS). - 2G (1990s): Digital voice + SMS (GSM, CDMA). - 3G (2000s): Mobile internet, video calling (UMTS, CDMA2000). - 4G (2010s): High-speed broadband, all-IP network (LTE). - 5G (2020s onward): Ultra-low latency, massive device connectivity, IoT support. Q6. What is a wireless network? Give two real-world examples. - A wireless network is a group of interconnected devices that communicate using radio waves instead of physical cables. - Example 1: Home/office Wi-Fi network connecting laptops and phones to the internet. - Example 2: Cellular mobile network connecting phones for calls and data. ### 5-Mark Questions Q1. Explain the basic communication system model with a neat diagram. \[Diagram: Block diagram — Source → Transmitter → Channel (with Noise) → Receiver → Destination\] - Information Source: generates the message (voice, data, image) to be communicated. - Transmitter: converts the message into a suitable electrical/electromagnetic signal (encoding + modulation). - Channel: the medium through which the signal travels; introduces attenuation, noise, and distortion. - Receiver: captures the transmitted signal and performs demodulation/decoding to recover the message. - Destination: the end user or device that receives the recovered information. - Noise source: models unwanted disturbances (thermal noise, interference) added during transmission. - The goal of system design is to maximize the fidelity of the recovered message despite channel impairments. Q2. Describe the evolution of wireless systems. How has the wireless spectrum evolved with each generation? - 1G: Analog FDMA systems, used low-frequency narrow bands (\~800–900 MHz), voice only. - 2G: Digital TDMA/CDMA systems, similar bands (900/1800 MHz), added SMS and low-rate data. - 3G: Introduced wider bands (1900–2100 MHz) using WCDMA/CDMA2000, enabling mobile internet. - 4G: Adopted OFDMA over broader bands (700 MHz–2.6 GHz) for high-speed all-IP data (LTE). - 5G: Uses sub-6 GHz and mmWave bands (24–100 GHz) for very high capacity and low latency. - Each generation demanded more spectrum and higher frequencies to support increasing data rates. - Spectrum reuse and efficient modulation techniques evolved alongside to use the limited spectrum better. Q3. Compare PAN, LAN, MAN, and WAN in terms of range, speed, and use cases. - PAN: Range \~10 m, speed up to a few Mbps, used for personal device connectivity (Bluetooth headsets, wearables). - LAN: Range \~100 m–1 km, speed up to Gbps (Wi-Fi 6/6E), used in homes, offices, campuses. - MAN: Range \~5–50 km, speed up to hundreds of Mbps, used for city-wide broadband (WiMAX, cable networks). - WAN: Range spans countries/continents, speed varies (Mbps–Gbps via backbone), used for cellular and satellite communication. - As range increases, achievable speed generally decreases due to path loss and shared infrastructure. - Choice of network type depends on coverage need, mobility, and cost. Q4. What are the major challenges in wireless communication? Explain any three in detail. - List: spectrum scarcity, multipath fading, interference, security, mobility management, power consumption. - **Spectrum scarcity:** usable frequency bands are limited and must be shared among many users/services, requiring careful allocation and reuse. - **Multipath fading:** signals reach the receiver via multiple reflected paths, causing constructive/destructive interference and signal strength fluctuation. - **Security:** since transmission is over open air, signals can be intercepted or jammed, requiring encryption and authentication. - Interference from co-channel and adjacent-channel users also degrades signal quality. - Mobility causes handoff challenges as users move between cells. - These challenges are addressed using techniques like diversity, encryption, and adaptive power control. Q5. Explain wireless spectrum and its regulations. Why is spectrum a limited resource? - Wireless spectrum refers to the band of electromagnetic frequencies allocated for wireless transmission. - It is limited because only certain frequency ranges propagate well and are practical for communication (e.g., RF band 3 kHz–300 GHz). - Higher frequencies suffer more attenuation and require line-of-sight, limiting usable ranges further. - Regulatory bodies (ITU internationally, national bodies like TRAI/FCC) allocate specific bands to specific services (broadcasting, cellular, defense, satellite). - Licensing and auctioning of spectrum ensures controlled, interference-free usage among operators. - Unlicensed bands (e.g., 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz) are reserved for shared use (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth) under power limits. - Because demand for wireless services keeps growing while spectrum remains fixed, efficient reuse (cellular concept, spectrum sharing) becomes essential. Q6. Explain the fundamental characteristics, advantages, limitations, and applications of wireless communication systems. - **Characteristics:** uses electromagnetic waves, supports mobility, time-varying and unpredictable channel behavior, broadcast in nature. - **Advantages:** mobility and flexibility for users, easy and low-cost deployment (no cabling), scalability, supports remote/inaccessible areas. - **Limitations:** limited bandwidth/spectrum, susceptible to interference and fading, lower security than wired links, higher power consumption at times. - **Applications:** mobile telephony, Wi-Fi internet access, satellite communication, IoT and sensor networks, broadcasting (radio/TV). - Wireless systems trade off reliability and security for the convenience of mobility and rapid deployment. --- ## MODULE 2 — Radio Propagation & Channel Modeling ### 2-Mark Questions Q1. State Friis transmission equation (free space propagation model). - Friis equation: Pr = Pt·Gt·Gr·(λ / 4πd)², relating received power (Pr) to transmitted power (Pt), antenna gains (Gt, Gr), wavelength (λ) and distance (d). - It applies to free-space (line-of-sight, no obstructions) propagation. - It shows received power decreases with the square of distance. Q2. What is path loss? How does it differ from shadowing? - Path loss is the reduction in signal power as it travels from transmitter to receiver, mainly due to distance (free-space spreading). - It is a large-scale, distance-dependent, deterministic effect. - Shadowing is an additional random signal loss caused by obstacles (buildings, terrain) blocking the path. - Shadowing varies randomly around the mean path loss and is modeled statistically (log-normal distribution). Q3. State Shannon's theorem. What does channel capacity depend on? - Shannon's theorem: C = B·log₂(1 + S/N), where C is channel capacity, B is bandwidth, S/N is signal-to-noise ratio. - It gives the maximum error-free data rate achievable over a noisy channel. - Capacity depends on available bandwidth and the SNR of the channel. Q4. What is the Doppler effect in wireless communication? Give the formula. - The Doppler effect is the apparent shift in received signal frequency caused by relative motion between transmitter and receiver. - Formula: fd = (v/c)·f0·cosθ, where v = relative velocity, c = speed of light, f0 = carrier frequency, θ = angle between motion and signal direction. - It causes frequency spreading and affects mobile receivers most at high speeds. Q5. Define multipath fading. What causes it? - Multipath fading is the rapid fluctuation in received signal amplitude/phase due to multiple copies of the signal arriving via different paths. - Caused by reflection, diffraction, and scattering off buildings, terrain, and other obstacles. - These multiple copies combine constructively or destructively at the receiver, causing fading. Q6. What is shadowing in a wireless channel? - Shadowing (large-scale fading) is signal attenuation caused by large obstacles like buildings or hills blocking the line of sight. - It causes slow, random variations in average received signal power over distance. - Statistically modeled using the log-normal distribution. Q7. State the Okumura model. In what scenario is it used? - The Okumura model is an empirical path-loss model based on extensive field measurements in Tokyo. - It predicts median path loss using correction factors for terrain, antenna height, and environment. - Used for urban macrocell prediction in the 150 MHz–1920 MHz frequency range, over distances of 1–100 km. Q8. How does the Hata model differ from the Okumura model? - The Hata model is a closed-form mathematical formula derived from Okumura's measurement data. - Unlike Okumura's graphical/tabular approach, Hata's model is easier to compute directly. - Hata model has defined sub-models for urban, suburban, and open (rural) areas. ### 5-Mark Questions Q1. Derive the free space path loss equation and explain each parameter. \[Diagram: Transmitter and receiver antenna separated by distance d, showing spreading wavefront\] - Start from Friis equation: Pr = Pt·Gt·Gr·(λ/4πd)². - Path loss (dB) is defined as PL = 10·log₁₀(Pt/Pr). - Substituting and simplifying gives: PL(dB) = 20log₁₀(d) + 20log₁₀(f) + 32.44 (with d in km, f in MHz). - Pt, Pr: transmitted and received power. - Gt, Gr: transmitter and receiver antenna gains. - λ: wavelength of the carrier signal; d: distance between transmitter and receiver. - The equation shows path loss increases with both distance and frequency, following an inverse-square power law. Q2. Compare the Okumura and Hata path loss models. When is each preferred? - Okumura: empirical, based on graphs/tables from field measurements in Tokyo; highly accurate but computationally cumbersome. - Hata: mathematical closed-form model derived from Okumura's data, easy to use in software/simulations. - Okumura covers 150 MHz–1920 MHz and distances 1–100 km; Hata is valid for similar ranges but simplified. - Hata provides separate formulas for urban, suburban, and rural areas. - Okumura is preferred when high accuracy from real measurement data is needed for detailed planning. - Hata is preferred for quick computer-based simulation and initial system design due to its simplicity. - Both are used for macrocellular outdoor coverage prediction. Q3. Explain shadowing and multipath fading with examples. How do they affect signal quality? \[Diagram: Transmitted signal reaching receiver via direct, reflected, and diffracted paths\] - Shadowing: large obstacles (buildings, hills) block or attenuate the signal, causing slow variation in average power. - Example: signal strength drops sharply when a mobile user moves behind a tall building. - Multipath fading: signal reaches the receiver through multiple paths (direct, reflected, diffracted, scattered) with different delays and phases. - Example: in a city, signals reflecting off buildings cause rapid fluctuations in received amplitude as a user walks. - Shadowing causes long-term (large-scale) signal variation; multipath fading causes short-term (small-scale) rapid variation. - Both degrade signal quality by reducing SNR and causing intersymbol interference, leading to higher bit error rates. - Diversity techniques and equalization are used to combat these effects. Q4. Derive and explain Shannon's channel capacity theorem. What is its significance? - Shannon's capacity theorem defines the theoretical maximum error-free data rate over a bandwidth-limited, noisy channel. - Formula: C = B·log₂(1 + S/N), where C = capacity (bits/sec), B = bandwidth (Hz), S/N = signal-to-noise ratio. - Derivation basis: the number of distinguishable signal levels is limited by noise; more levels allow more bits per symbol. - As S/N increases, more information can be reliably packed into the same bandwidth. - Significance: sets an upper bound on achievable data rate — no coding scheme can exceed this limit reliably. - It guides system design trade-offs between bandwidth and transmit power. - It forms the theoretical basis for modern modulation and coding scheme design (e.g., choosing QAM order based on available SNR). Q5. Explain the Doppler effect in wireless communication with the formula. How does it impact moving receivers? \[Diagram: Moving receiver approaching/receding from transmitter with velocity vector and angle θ\] - Doppler effect: apparent change in received frequency due to relative motion between transmitter and receiver. - Formula: fd = (v/c)·f0·cosθ, where v is relative velocity, c is speed of light, f0 is carrier frequency, θ is angle of motion relative to signal path. - Positive Doppler shift occurs when receiver moves toward the transmitter; negative shift when moving away. - Impact: causes frequency spreading (Doppler spread) of the received signal spectrum. - At high vehicle speeds, this can cause inter-carrier interference in OFDM-based systems. - It affects channel coherence time — faster movement means the channel changes more rapidly, requiring frequent channel estimation. - Systems compensate using frequency tracking, robust modulation, and adaptive equalization. Q6. Describe channel capacity and its relationship with bandwidth and SNR using Shannon's theorem. - Channel capacity (C) is the theoretical maximum rate at which information can be transmitted error-free over a channel. - Shannon's formula: C = B·log₂(1 + S/N). - Increasing bandwidth (B) increases capacity linearly (more Hz to carry information). - Increasing SNR increases capacity logarithmically — diminishing returns at high SNR. - There is a fundamental trade-off: a system can use wide bandwidth with lower power, or narrow bandwidth with higher power, to achieve similar capacity. - In practice, real systems operate below Shannon capacity due to implementation constraints (imperfect coding, hardware limitations). - This relationship guides spectrum-efficient design in modern systems (e.g., 5G uses wide bandwidth + high-order modulation to push capacity higher). Q7. Derive the Friis transmission equation and explain the significance of each parameter. \[Diagram: Isotropic transmitter radiating power over a sphere of radius d, receiver antenna capturing effective area\] - Consider an isotropic antenna radiating power Pt uniformly over a sphere of radius d; power density at receiver = Pt / 4πd². - With transmit antenna gain Gt, power density becomes Pt·Gt / 4πd². - Receiver antenna has effective aperture Ae = Gr·λ² / 4π. - Received power: Pr = (Pt·Gt / 4πd²) × Ae = Pt·Gt·Gr·(λ/4πd)². - Pt: transmitted power; Gt, Gr: antenna gains of transmitter and receiver. - λ: signal wavelength (related to frequency: λ = c/f). - d: distance between transmitter and receiver — received power falls off as 1/d². - This equation is the foundation for link budget calculations in wireless system design. Q8. Discuss the Okumura and Hata propagation models in detail, including the scenarios in which each is preferred. - Okumura model: developed from extensive signal strength measurements in and around Tokyo; results presented as correction-factor graphs. - It accounts for terrain irregularity, antenna height gain, street orientation, and urban/suburban/open area corrections. - Valid for frequencies 150 MHz–1920 MHz, distances 1–100 km, base station heights 30–1000 m. - Hata model: mathematical formulation of Okumura's graphical data, giving direct closed-form equations for path loss. - Provides separate equations for urban, suburban, and open rural areas. - Hata's simplicity makes it suitable for computer simulation and automated network planning tools. - Okumura is preferred for detailed, high-accuracy manual planning; Hata is preferred for rapid, software-based estimation. - Both models are widely used for macrocellular system coverage and interference planning. Q9. Derive the Doppler frequency equation and explain its significance for moving receivers. \[Diagram: Vector triangle showing receiver velocity v, direction of motion, and incoming wave angle θ\] - Consider a receiver moving with velocity v at angle θ relative to the direction of the incoming signal from the transmitter. - The path length difference traveled per unit time causes an apparent frequency shift. - Change in path length per second = v·cosθ; corresponding phase change relates to wavelength λ. - Doppler shift: fd = (v·cosθ)/λ = (v/c)·f0·cosθ (since λ = c/f0). - Maximum Doppler shift occurs when θ = 0° (motion directly toward/away from transmitter): fd(max) = v·f0/c. - Significance: determines the Doppler spread and coherence time of the wireless channel. - High-speed mobile users (e.g., in vehicles/trains) experience larger Doppler shifts, requiring the receiver to track frequency changes for correct demodulation. Q10. Explain the effect of bandwidth and SNR on Shannon's channel capacity, with reference to the capacity formula. - Capacity formula: C = B·log₂(1 + S/N). - Effect of bandwidth: capacity increases directly (linearly) with B — doubling bandwidth roughly doubles capacity (for fixed SNR). - However, increasing bandwidth also increases the amount of noise power captured, which can limit the actual gain. - Effect of SNR: capacity increases logarithmically with SNR — large increases in power give only modest capacity gains at high SNR. - At very high SNR, capacity becomes bandwidth-limited; at very low SNR (noise-limited), capacity becomes power-limited. - This shows two ways to increase capacity: widen the bandwidth or improve SNR (increase power/reduce noise), with bandwidth being the more efficient lever at high rates. - Real-world systems (5G, Wi-Fi 6) exploit both wider bandwidths and higher-order modulation (requiring higher SNR) to approach Shannon limits. Q11. Explain the different radio propagation mechanisms — reflection, diffraction, and scattering — and their effect on signal propagation. \[Diagram: Wave hitting a smooth wall (reflection), an edge/corner (diffraction), and rough surface/foliage (scattering)\] - **Reflection:** occurs when a radio wave strikes a smooth surface (much larger than wavelength) like a building wall or ground, bouncing the wave in a new direction. - Reflection causes multiple signal copies to reach the receiver, contributing to multipath fading. - **Diffraction:** occurs when a wave bends around an obstacle's edge (like a building corner or hilltop), allowing signals to reach receivers even without direct line of sight. - Diffraction explains why signals can still be received in shadowed regions behind obstacles, though with reduced strength. - **Scattering:** occurs when a wave hits a rough surface or small objects (foliage, lamp posts, irregular surfaces) comparable to or smaller than the wavelength, spreading energy in many directions. - Scattering generally weakens the signal further and adds random small-scale fading components. - Together, these three mechanisms explain the multipath nature of real-world wireless channels and are the basis for statistical channel/fading models. --- — End of Module 1 & 2 Solutions — Module 3 to follow —
